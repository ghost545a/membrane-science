1 · Introduction

What a membrane is, and why it works

A membrane is a selective barrier between two phases. Under a driving force, some species pass through it faster than others, and that difference in rate is the whole of membrane separation.

A short history

In 1748 Abbé Nollet saw water move through a pig's bladder into wine: the first recorded osmosis. Adolf Fick wrote his law of diffusion in 1855, Thomas Graham studied gas permeation through rubber in 1866, and van 't Hoff gave osmotic pressure its thermodynamic form in 1887. Frederick Donnan explained in 1911 why fixed charges in a gel keep out ions of the same sign. The field became an industry in the early 1960s, when Sidney Loeb and Srinivasa Sourirajan cast the first asymmetric cellulose acetate membrane: a dense skin about 0.1–0.2 µm thick on a porous support, thin enough to make reverse osmosis desalination practical. Synthetic ion-exchange membranes (Juda and McRae, 1950) made electrodialysis possible, and in 1954 R. E. Pattle showed that the same membranes can generate electricity from salt and fresh water, the idea behind this whole site.

Driving forces

Every membrane process is defined by what pushes the permeating species across. The flux of a species \(i\) is proportional to the gradient of its chemical (or electrochemical) potential \(\mu_i\):

$$J_i = -L_i\,\frac{d\mu_i}{dx},\qquad \mu_i = \mu_i^0 + RT\ln a_i + \bar V_i\,p + z_iF\varphi$$

\(a_i\) activity, \(p\) pressure, \(\varphi\) electric potential, \(z_i\) charge number. Each term is one family of processes.

Pore flow

In porous membranes the solvent flows through capillaries; for straight cylindrical pores the Hagen–Poiseuille equation gives the flux:

$$J = \frac{\varepsilon\, r^2}{8\,\eta\,\tau}\,\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta x}$$

\(\varepsilon\) porosity, \(r\) pore radius, \(\eta\) viscosity, \(\tau\) tortuosity, \(\Delta x\) thickness.

Solution–diffusion

Dense membranes have no permanent pores. A molecule dissolves in the polymer at the feed face, diffuses down its concentration gradient and leaves at the other face. Separation comes from differences in both solubility and diffusivity. For reverse osmosis this gives a water flux driven by the pressure in excess of the osmotic pressure, and a salt flux that depends only on the concentration difference:

$$J_w = A\,(\Delta p - \Delta\pi),\qquad J_s = B\,(c_f - c_p),\qquad \pi \approx i\,c\,RT$$

With the van 't Hoff relation, sea water (≈0.6 M NaCl equivalent) has an osmotic pressure of roughly 27 bar. That is the same free energy RED and PRO try to recover.

Diffusion: Fick's first law

When only a concentration difference acts, the flux through a membrane of thickness \(\delta\) at pseudo-steady state is:

$$J = -D\,\frac{dc}{dx} \;\approx\; D\,\frac{\Delta c}{\delta}$$

This is the equation we used to get the apparent salt diffusion coefficient of our membrane in section 3.

Ion-exchange membranes

Ion-exchange membranes (IEMs) are dense polymer films carrying fixed charged groups at high concentration, typically 1–3 mmol per gram of dry polymer. The fixed charge is balanced by mobile counter-ions of the opposite sign, which can move through the swollen polymer. Ions with the same sign as the fixed charge, the co-ions, are pushed out.

  • Cation exchange membranes (CEM) carry fixed negative groups, usually sulfonic acid (−SO₃⁻). They pass Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and repel Cl⁻.
  • Anion exchange membranes (AEM) carry fixed positive groups, usually quaternary ammonium (−NR₃⁺). They pass Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻ and repel Na⁺. Our Fumasep® FAA-3-PK-75 is an AEM.

Donnan equilibrium and the membrane potential

At the interface between a solution and an IEM, the electrochemical potential of each mobile ion is equal on both sides. Because the membrane already holds a high concentration of counter-ions, an interfacial potential, the Donnan potential, builds up and keeps co-ions out:

$$E_{Don} = \frac{RT}{z_iF}\ln\frac{a_i^{s}}{a_i^{m}}$$

Put a perfectly selective membrane between two solutions of different activity and the two Donnan potentials no longer cancel. The difference is the membrane potential, given by the Nernst equation and scaled by the apparent permselectivity \(\alpha\):

$$E_m = \alpha\,\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{a_{c}}{a_{d}},\qquad \alpha = \frac{t^{m}_{ct} - t^{s}_{ct}}{1 - t^{s}_{ct}}$$

\(t_{ct}^m\), \(t_{ct}^s\): transport numbers of the counter-ion in the membrane and in the solution. \(\alpha = 1\) is an ideal membrane; co-ion leakage lowers it.

The properties that matter

  • Permselectivity α: how well co-ions are excluded. Falls at high salt concentration, as Donnan exclusion weakens.
  • Area resistance (Ω·cm²): the ohmic loss per unit area. Good RED membranes are around 1–3 Ω·cm².
  • Ion-exchange capacity and water uptake: more fixed charge lowers resistance but swells the polymer, which dilutes the fixed charge and costs selectivity.
  • Monovalent selectivity: preference for Cl⁻ over SO₄²⁻, or Na⁺ over Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺. Central to our project (section 2).
  • Thickness: thinner means lower resistance but more diffusion of salt and water.

Electrodialysis and its reverse

In electrodialysis an applied voltage drives ions through a stack of alternating CEMs and AEMs, so that every second compartment is depleted of salt. Reverse electrodialysis runs the same stack backwards: feed a concentrated and a dilute solution into alternating compartments and the ions diffuse on their own, cations through the CEMs one way and anions through the AEMs the other way. The separated charge is a voltage, and redox reactions at the end electrodes turn it into an electron current through an external load.

What goes wrong in practice

  • Concentration polarisation: the solution next to the membrane is depleted or enriched compared with the bulk, which lowers the effective driving force. Stirring, spacers and higher velocity thin out this boundary layer, which is why both compartments in our cell were stirred.
  • Fouling: organic matter, colloids, biofilms and scale (CaCO₃, Mg(OH)₂) block the membrane and spacers. Natural waters need pretreatment.
  • Multivalent ions: Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ raise membrane resistance and can move against their own gradient ("uphill transport"), cutting the voltage.

For a full treatment, see Mulder's Basic Principles of Membrane Technology and Tanaka's Ion Exchange Membranes.

2 · Blue energy

Power from where rivers meet the sea

Wherever a river flows into the sea, fresh and salt water mix and release free energy as heat. Reverse electrodialysis captures part of it as electricity instead.

Mixing a cubic metre of river water into the sea releases roughly 2–3 MJ of Gibbs free energy (published estimates differ with their assumptions), about the same as that water falling from a dam 200–300 m high. Counted over all the world's rivers, the theoretical potential of salinity gradient energy is about 2.6 TW; once technical and physical limits are included, it falls to about 983 GW [1]. That is well beyond tidal energy and comparable to wave energy [1, 2].

How a RED stack works

A RED stack is a pile of cell pairs. Each pair is one CEM, one concentrated compartment, one AEM and one dilute compartment, with spacers holding the 100–300 µm gaps open. Na⁺ leaves the sea water through the CEM toward one electrode; Cl⁻ leaves through the AEM toward the other. The voltages of all membranes add up in series.

$$E_{OCV} = N\,\frac{RT}{F}\left(\alpha_{CEM} + \alpha_{AEM}\right)\ln\frac{a_{sea}}{a_{river}},\qquad P_{max} = \frac{E_{OCV}^2}{4\,R_{stack}}$$

With \(N\) cell pairs, sea water at about 0.5 M and river water at about 0.017 M, an ideal cell pair gives roughly 0.17 V. Power peaks when the external load equals the internal resistance of the stack. Performance is reported as power density, in W per m² of membrane.

Why monovalent selectivity matters

Real sea water is not pure NaCl. Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ raise the membrane's resistance and, because one divalent ion can swap places with two monovalent ones, they can move against their own gradient and reduce the voltage. In pilot plants fed with natural water, this has cut power to about half of what synthetic NaCl solutions give [1]. Membranes that favour Cl⁻ (or Na⁺) over multivalent ions should keep the power up. Selectivity can come from electrostatic repulsion, size exclusion, differences in hydration energy and mobility, and surface modification of the membrane [2].

Before any of that can be tested, we need a baseline: how chloride, a single monovalent ion, moves out of NaCl solution through a standard anion exchange membrane when only a concentration difference drives it. That is our experiment, and the first step toward testing multivalent ions and selectivity.

Method

A two-compartment diffusion cell was used to study ion transport through the Fumasep® FAA-3-PK-75 anion exchange membrane. The membrane, with an effective diameter of 2.6 cm (an exposed area of 5.31 cm²), was clamped between the two compartments, separating them completely. One side held 300 mL of 0.1 M NaCl as the feed; the other held 300 mL of deionised water as the receiver. Both were stirred magnetically the whole time, to keep the concentration uniform and limit concentration polarisation at the membrane surfaces.

The conductivity of both sides was recorded every 30 minutes for 180 minutes, with the temperature taken at each sampling point, under ambient laboratory conditions. Before the test, the membrane was soaked in 0.1 M NaCl to hydrate it and convert it to the chloride form.

Equipment

  • Two-compartment diffusion cell
  • Fumasep® FAA-3-PK-75 anion exchange membrane (75 µm, 2.6 cm exposed diameter)
  • Two magnetic stirrers
  • Conductivity meter with a probe and built-in thermometer

Results

Receiver conductivity was converted to concentration with Kohlrausch's law, using a molar conductivity of 98 S·cm²/mol for NaCl at about 20 °C. Conductivity counts every ion present, so the result is the concentration of salt that crossed: Cl⁻ together with its partner cation. In the very dilute receiver the molar conductivity of NaCl is somewhat higher, up to about 126 S·cm²/mol at infinite dilution and 25 °C, so these concentrations, and D, may be up to about 20% lower; a calibration line with NaCl standards will pin this down in the next runs.

$$c\;(\text{mmol/L}) = \frac{\kappa\;(\mu\text{S/cm})}{\Lambda_m},\qquad \Lambda_m = \lambda_{Na^+} + \lambda_{Cl^-} \approx 98\ \text{S}\cdot\text{cm}^2/\text{mol}$$

The flux and the diffusion coefficient then follow from Fick's first law at pseudo-steady state, with ΔcR = 0.612 mmol/L after Δt = 10 800 s, V = 0.300 L, A = 5.307 cm², δ = 75 µm and Δc = 0.1 mol/L:

$$J = \frac{\Delta c_R\,V}{A\,\Delta t} = \frac{(6.12\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L})(0.300\ \text{L})}{(5.307\ \text{cm}^2)(10\,800\ \text{s})} = 3.20\times10^{-9}\ \text{mol cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$$ $$D = \frac{J\,\delta}{\Delta c} = \frac{(3.20\times10^{-9})(7.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{cm})}{1.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol/cm}^3} = 2.40\times10^{-7}\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s} = 2.40\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$$

This calculation uses the 180-minute point. A least-squares line through all seven points gives D = 2.24 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s (95% confidence interval about 1.5–3.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s, R² = 0.93), close to the end-point value. Its small positive intercept (0.08 mmol/L) suggests a quick early release, most likely salt held in the membrane after pretreatment. D here is an apparent coefficient for the salt: it includes the stirred boundary layers and the partitioning of salt into the membrane, and it is based on the nominal 75 µm thickness.

$$S\,(\%) = \left(1 - \frac{c_R}{c_F}\right)\times 100,\qquad \eta\,(\%) = \frac{c_R}{c_{F}}\times 100$$

S stayed above 99.3% while η rose from 0.26% to 0.62%. Both describe how slowly the salt moves: after three hours the cell is still far from equilibrium, which is what a dense, strongly charged membrane should show. Separating the two ions, the next step, will turn these rates into a measure of selectivity.

Conclusions

  • Chloride transport through the Fumasep® FAA-3-PK-75 anion exchange membrane was characterised at zero current, by conductivity.
  • The apparent diffusion coefficient was 2.40 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s from the end point (2.24 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s from a fit to all points): slow, steady transport driven by the concentration gradient.
  • After 180 minutes only 0.62% of the feed concentration had reached the receiver, consistent with strong Donnan exclusion of the Na⁺ co-ion limiting how fast salt can leak through.
  • These values are the baseline for the 2026–27 work.

Next steps

The next experiments build directly on this baseline. Measuring Cl⁻ (by titration or an ion-selective electrode) and Na⁺ (by ICP-OES or AAS) separately in the receiver will show how much of each ion crosses, and membrane-potential measurements will give the transport number and permselectivity. A conductivity calibration line, temperature control (the solutions warmed from about 16 °C to 21 °C during this run) and at least three repeat runs with a blank cell will put an uncertainty on D. Following both bottles will also close the salt balance: in this run the feed lost about 0.58 mmol of NaCl while the receiver gained 0.18 mmol.

Then come the questions this project was set up to answer: adding Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ to the feed to test monovalent selectivity, and measuring area resistance and power density in a RED stack (section 5).

Who has tried this at scale

RED has been tested at pilot scale but is not yet commercial [1]. These four projects show how far it has come, and what still holds it back.

The 32 km Afsluitdijk dam already separates fresh and salt water, so both feeds sit side by side. The pilot uses buffer tanks, pretreatment filters and membrane stacks, building on a decade of RED research at Wetsus. Detailed operating data has not been made public [1]. In 2023 a Dutch trade magazine reported €5 million from the Wadden Fund to expand the site.

Instead of sea water, REAPower used saturated brine from the saltworks against brackish water, for a much larger salinity ratio. Filters at 50, 25 and 5 µm and twice-weekly shock chlorination handled fouling. But the power was only about half of what synthetic NaCl gave: the divalent ions in natural brine cost the other half [1].

Pressure-retarded osmosis (not RED)

Norway

Tofte, Hurum · Statkraft with SINTEF · 2009–2013

Design rating
10 kW; 2–4 kW reported (SINTEF: 2 kW)
Membrane

Target
5 W/m² to be economic

The world's first osmotic power plant, opened in November 2009. It used the other salinity-gradient route: in PRO, water crosses a semi-permeable membrane into pressurised sea water and the extra volume drives a turbine. The membranes never reached the power density needed, and Statkraft stopped its osmotic power work in 2013.

PRO vs RED: PRO moves water and needs high-pressure pumps, turbines and pressure exchangers; RED moves ions and gives electricity directly at low pressure. Tofte's lesson for RED is the same one: membrane power density and cost decide everything.

Japan

Okinawa, beside a seawater RO plant · Yamaguchi University

The Yamaguchi group (Higa and co-workers) used the concentrated brine from a desalination plant as the salty feed, which both raises the power and puts a waste stream to use. They fitted monovalent-selective membranes (Neosepta ACS-8T and CIMS). Compared with synthetic NaCl, divalent ions cut the power by about 36%, against about 50% in Italy [1]. The review credits the monovalent-selective membranes for the smaller loss. The two pilots also differed in feeds, site and stack design, so this is a likely explanation rather than a controlled comparison, and it is the question our project works toward.

Across all four, the same limits come back: membrane power density, fouling with natural water, multivalent ions, and membrane cost. Our project aims at the third: understanding monovalent transport so that membranes can be chosen, or made, to let Cl⁻ and Na⁺ through while holding back Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and SO₄²⁻.
